# When Re-Prompting Adds No New Evidence

In *The Cursed Museum*, a hotspot labelled “painted round-topped chest” sat over an ottoman. Coordinate changes produced different boxes, but no better way to tell a near miss from a correct outline. The workflow could generate candidates, but it could not evaluate them reliably. My claim is that repeated retries contribute little to reliable improvement unless they produce evidence that helps distinguish a near miss from success. Otherwise, the uncertain decision should move into a representation a person can inspect. Once verified, the parts that can be expressed as rules should enter the harness.

Deliberate practice requires activities designed to improve performance rather than repetition alone ([Ericsson et al., 1993](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.100.3.363)). [Schön (1983, p. 79)](https://books.google.com/books/about/Reflective_Practitioner.html?id=oYNHAAAAMAAJ) describes design as a conversation in which the situation “talks back.” Surprise prompts reframing. Ericsson explains when repetition counts, while Schön suggests reframing when it does not. For agents, this means improving the feedback that separates a near miss from success.

Coding-assistant studies suggest this problem by analogy. In [Vaithilingam et al.’s (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3491101.3519665) study, 19 of 24 participants preferred Copilot to ordinary IntelliSense autocomplete, although Copilot did not necessarily improve task time or success. Participants struggled to understand and debug generated code. In a 2025 preprint, 16 experienced developers predicted a 24% speed-up using early-2025 AI tools but took 19% longer ([Becker et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089)). Neither study directly examines re-prompting. They suggest how fluent output can conceal evaluation and repair costs. Becker et al. acknowledge that better scaffolding may improve productivity. Yet even when AI saves time, speed alone cannot establish whether its output is correct.

The repository records three agent-assisted hotspot corrections on 28–29 October. The second notes that the first still left several outlines misplaced. A later audit found that 34 of 72 shapes were four-point placeholders. I retraced them, then built an editor for later work. Its colour-threshold method split the paintings’ shadows into fragments, so I replaced it with SlimSAM. My loose box prompts a mask, which becomes a polygon whose points I can move over the painting. The mask is model output. The polygon is the intermediate representation I inspect.

SlimSAM changed what I could evaluate, not just what the agent could generate. Coordinates asked me to trust the agent’s reading of the painting. An editable overlay let me compare its boundary directly with the object. This separated two tasks that repeated prompting had blurred. One was generating a candidate; the other was deciding whether to accept it. The spec now checks the accepted decision’s form by rejecting the old `{x, y, width, height}` schema. It does not check whether a polygon follows the correct object. I still judge that from the overlay.

This sharpens [Böckeler’s (2026)](https://martinfowler.com/articles/harness-engineering.html) steering loop. A recurring issue should improve the harness’s guides and sensors rather than trigger more retries. Her sensors include review agents, not only deterministic tests. I infer that a reviewer and proposer can share the same blind spot when both use similar visual inputs and criteria. In the museum, I first had to decide that a hotspot should follow the painted object’s contour rather than a surrounding box. Only then could a test forbid the old schema. Böckeler is a Thoughtworks engineer proposing a practice model rather than reporting a controlled comparison. Her account explains why the correction should outlast one session.

[Willison (n.d.)](https://simonwillison.net/guides/agentic-engineering-patterns/agentic-manual-testing/) offers a useful counter-case. He has agents inspect browser screenshots but also wants to see a feature work before release. He developed Showboat to record agent testing, so his account also promotes his own tool. My rotation episode supports both positions. The recorded agent task concerned clipping, and CI passed its shrink-to-fit fix. No video or frame sequence entered the harness. I rejected the fix only after dragging the dial and watching the relic pulse in size. An agent could inspect that behaviour if given a temporal signal and a constant-size criterion. But that criterion came from my own inspection. This exposes a gap in my workflow, not a permanent limit of machine vision.

Good agentic development should measure progress by whether each attempt provides better evidence of correctness. Before retrying, I ask what new check could distinguish a near miss from success. If I cannot name one, I change the observation method or representation. Agents can remain autonomous where success is observable and errors are reliably detected. Where judgement remains uncertain, I place the output in a form a person can inspect. I then encode the verified, expressible decisions in the harness so the same mistakes are less likely to recur.
